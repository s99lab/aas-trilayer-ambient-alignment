# Part III Summary

## Title

**Conditions and Limits of Structural Redescription for Record-Deficient Cases**

---

## Core Proposition

Part III defines when structural redescription is permissible and when it must stop.

The paper addresses cases where original interaction records are incomplete, missing, or only partially available.

Its central claim is that record-deficient cases can sometimes be structurally redescribed, but only under strict limits.

Part III also clarifies an important boundary for long-horizon human-AI work: when records are incomplete, AAS should not over-attribute the origin of concepts, intentions, or causal pathways. It should describe what can be supported by surviving records and stop where the archive no longer supports stronger claims.

---

## Role in the Series

Part III is the boundary-discipline layer of the AAS series.

Part I defines the framework.

Part II operationalizes AAS as a classifiable interaction state.

Part III prevents the framework from overreaching when evidence is incomplete.

Part IV / Extension builds on this by reconstructing a longitudinal case from a bounded archive.

Part III is especially important for later AAS development because it provides the stopping rules needed for mixed concept formation and governance-of-formation analysis.

## Key Concepts

### Structural Redescription

Structural redescription means describing the observable structure of an interaction from surviving artifacts.

It does not mean reconstructing every original event, intention, or causal pathway.

### Record-Deficient Cases

A record-deficient case is one in which the original interaction records are incomplete, unavailable, fragmented, or partially reconstructed.

Such cases require stricter interpretive discipline.

### Stopping Rules

Part III emphasizes that some claims must not be made when records are insufficient.

This is central to the integrity of the AAS framework.

### Origin Attribution Limits

Origin attribution limits refer to the boundary against assigning a concept, claim, or structure to a single hidden origin when the archive does not support that conclusion.

In long-horizon human-AI work, it may be difficult or impossible to determine whether a concept was fully present in the human author's mind before AI-assisted verbalization, or whether it emerged through the interaction itself.

Part III treats this uncertainty as a reason for stronger audit discipline, not as permission for narrative completion.

### Formation Governance

Formation governance focuses on how a concept was adopted, rejected, revised, bounded, recorded, and made publishable.

When exact origin attribution is unavailable, AAS can still examine whether the process of formation remained auditable.

The key question becomes not only where a concept came from, but how it was judged, stabilized, bounded, and made responsible.

---

## What This Paper Does

Part III defines the conditions under which a case may be redescribed from limited evidence.

It provides guardrails for distinguishing legitimate structural description from overinterpretation.

It clarifies that structural redescription should remain tied to observable artifacts, preserved records, and explicit limits.

It also supports a safer approach to long-horizon human-AI concept formation: when the origin of a concept cannot be cleanly assigned to either the human or the AI system, the analysis should focus on observable formation governance rather than hidden origin claims.

---

## What This Paper Does Not Claim

Part III does not claim that missing records can be fully recovered.

It does not claim that reconstruction can access hidden states.

It does not prove the original case in a strong causal sense.

It does not permit narrative completion where evidence is absent.

It does not convert partial records into total history.

It does not claim that the exact origin of every concept, phrase, or structure can be recovered from incomplete records.

It does not claim that AI independently authored, invented, or owned a concept where the archive does not support that claim.

It also does not claim that every final concept must have existed fully formed in the human author's mind before AI-assisted verbalization.

Instead, Part III preserves a disciplined middle position: where origin cannot be reconstructed, the analysis should stop at observable formation, adoption, revision, responsibility, and revision conditions.
